## Industry-Wide Standards

- ISO 6486-1:2019
The global standard for ceramic ware, glass-ceramic ware, and glass dinnerware in contact with food. It specifically outlines the test methods for measuring the release of lead and cadmium.

- ASTM C927
The standard test method evaluating the physical durability of glass and ceramic tableware, covering thermal shock resistance, impact resistance, and edge/rim chip resistance.

- ASTM C738-94
The standardized laboratory testing method for extracting and quantifying lead and cadmium from glazed ceramic surfaces to ensure food safety compliance (such as FDA approvals).

- ASTM D3363:
The standard test method for determining the surface hardness of the glaze using the "pencil hardness" physical scratch test.



## Ceramics quality checks:

1. Mechanical Strength (3-Point Bending) Test

Structural integrity is critical for ceramic cutlery. The item is placed across two supports, and an increasing downward force is applied directly to the center until it fractures. This determines the maximum load the ceramic can bear without snapping.

2. Thermal Shock Resistance Test

This simulates rapid, extreme temperature changes to ensure the ceramic does not crack during washing or food serving. The cutlery is heated to a high temperature (typically 150°C–200°C) and then immediately plunged into cold water (5°C–10°C).

3. Edge Impact and Chip Resistance Test

A swinging pendulum or controlled weight strikes the edge or rim of the ceramic. This test measures the exact energy (in joules) required to cause a visible chip, ensuring the cutlery can withstand accidental knocks during everyday use.

4. Glaze Hardness and Scratch Resistance Test

Using the Mohs hardness scale or specific pencil hardness tools, the surface is dragged and scratched. This confirms the glaze will not easily scratch, which would otherwise harbor bacteria or degrade visually over time.

5. Acoustic (Sound) Testing

An inspector gently strikes the ceramic piece. A clear, crisp, and resonant sound indicates a structurally sound and well-fired piece. A dull or hoarse sound immediately points to hidden internal fractures, hairline cracks, or improper firing.

6. Heavy Metal Leaching Test

The cutlery is filled or submerged in an acetic acid solution for 24 hours. The liquid is then tested to measure if the glaze leaches harmful toxins like lead or cadmium into food.

7. Water Absorption Test

The ceramic is weighed dry, boiled in water, and weighed again. High-quality vitreous ceramics and hard-paste porcelains must have a water absorption rate of less than 0.5% to prevent moisture penetration, cracking, and bacterial growth.
